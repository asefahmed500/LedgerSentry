Finance teams at small businesses check the same two things every month: did the payments we received match the invoices we sent, and do vendor contracts actually follow our procurement policy.

Rule-based systems handle the clean eighty percent and then go quiet. The messy twenty percent — partial payments, misspelled vendor names, scanned invoices, clauses buried in prose — is where a human squints at two PDFs side by side, and where money quietly leaks.

This is LedgerSentry. One agent, two jobs: reconcile payments against invoices, and check every contract clause against the rulebook — with a confidence score and full evidence on every decision.

Everything runs against a real Postgres ledger, inside a JWT-protected reviewer workspace.

Here is the baseline versus the agent. Invoice 0071 from Dhaka Textiles Mills: the strict baseline matcher says unmatched. The agent says matched, ninety-five percent confidence — and when we open the details, it explains exactly why: transaction 0032, exact amount, date within window, vendor resolved.

Uploading a real scanned invoice takes three steps. Pick the scan — OCR reads it automatically. Review the fields it extracted — these come from tesseract, and the ledger record stays the source of truth. Optionally link the purchase order this invoice bills against. Confirm and upload.

Then run the agent. It investigates: it reads the document, searches the payment feed, scores the vendor name, and commits to a decision with its evidence.

Every step is logged. This is the agent trajectory — the exact tool calls, inputs, outputs, and reasoning. These JSON files ship with the repo as the audit trail.

Compliance works the same way. Purchase orders are checked against the rulebook — and every violation quotes the exact clause it conflicts with.

When the agent is unsure, it doesn't guess. This ambiguous warranty clause was routed to the human review queue with the agent's reasoning attached. A human approves or rejects — the agent only recommends.

And it's measured, not vibes. One command scores the strict baseline against the agent on the same labeled dataset: match accuracy, false-positive rate, partial-payment handling, compliance precision and recall. The numbers live in the database, not a screenshot.

The changelog tells the story: from a strict rule baseline at seventy-five percent accuracy, through fuzzy matching, partial-payment detection, OCR with fallback, and ambiguity routing — including the experiments we removed and why.

The main failure mode is OCR on bad scans — so extraction confidence flows into match confidence, and uncertainty routes to a human. Because the agent's most valuable output isn't the match. It's a reasoned "I'm not sure."
